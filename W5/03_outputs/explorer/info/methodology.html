How the numbers were made

Sensor-based recyclables collection in Rio Maior, Portugal · operator data January 2020 – April 2024 · working round W5 · back to the explorer

The explorer draws on two files handed over by the collection operator: the drivers' electronic logbook and the readings of the fill sensors mounted inside part of the bins. Neither file can be used as delivered. This page explains, step by step and in plain language, what was done to each file, what was removed and why, and which rules stand behind every number on the screen. It is written so that a jury member can check any figure against the named source files without reading code.

1The two files, and the accounting promise

Rio Maior has 816 recycling bins for packaging, paper/card and glass. When a crew empties one, the driver presses a button; the logbook stores the bin, the moment, and a route identifier called idrecolha (the run number the crew keys in). At the end of the run the truck is weighed, so each identifier also carries a total load in kg and a total distance in km. That file has 264,817 rows covering January 2020 to April 2024.

Separately, 344 of the 816 bins carry an ultrasonic fill sensor (a device that measures how full the bin is, several times a day). Its export holds 1,048,575 readings. That number is exactly the row ceiling of an Excel sheet, which means the export was cut off there, not that the sensors stopped; this truncation is logged as defect D1 in the project's defect register. Glass is essentially uninstrumented: 2 sensors.

The central problem is that most rows in the driver file are not emptying records at all, and one identifier is often not one trip. Untangled, every one of the 264,817 rows ends up in exactly one of eight places:

TierRowsWhat it is

I120,813 Inferred pass-by: an unstamped reading placed on the one vehicle track that could feasibly have produced it (win probability ≥ 0.7). An observation of the bin, never a collection.
L7,807 Low-confidence placement (below 0.7): more than one track fits. Drawn with a question mark.
P10,136 Phantom stop: a reading no logged truck could reach, chained with others into 1,674 reconstructed vehicle tracks (drawn dotted).

Total264,817Every raw row, exactly once.
Nothing was silently discarded. A row that does not appear on the map sits in a companion workbook with a stated reason, and the eight tiers above sum to the raw row count without remainder.

Two rules hold everywhere in the explorer and in the thesis. First, inferred, low-confidence, phantom and isolated rows are observations, not collections; no statistic ever counts them as an emptying. Second, the weighbridge load and the recorded kilometres describe a whole identifier. When an identifier turns out to contain several vehicles, its kg and km are shown with each track but summed once. Totals built this way: 25,724 tonnes and 1,308,593 km over the four years and four months.

2From button presses to drivable vehicle tracks

Treating each idrecolha as one trip fails on physical grounds. Some identifiers stamp two bins seconds apart that lie 20 km from each other; one recorded 54 bins in a single minute, spread over 20.8 km. Crews share numbers, run two trucks under one code, or type the whole day in at the desk after the shift. So every identifier was tested against the road network, and split or repaired until each consecutive pair of stamps is physically drivable by a real truck.

The road-time engine

Distances and times come from the OpenStreetMap road network around Rio Maior (13,204 road segments; footpaths and stairs excluded), built into a routable graph of 236,364 nodes in the Portuguese national grid (EPSG:3763). The 816 bins collapse to 270 kerbside sites (bins standing together share a site), plus the depot and the transfer station: 272 sites, snapped to the nearest road with a median error of 7 m. Between every pair of sites the engine stores two travel times. The legal time uses the speed a loaded truck may actually drive on each road class (90 km/h on motorway, 80 on trunk, primary and secondary roads, 70 on tertiary, 50–60 on links and everything else). The ceiling time uses the physical limit of the road (130/100/100/90/80, 60 otherwise) and exists only to judge doubtful cases. No flat average speed appears anywhere in the pipeline.

Service time and tolerance, measured rather than assumed

How long does a stop take? The data answer: within clean single-vehicle trips, the median gap between consecutive stamps at different sites leaves 0.02 minutes beyond the driving time, measured over 12,587 stamp pairs in 8,837 trusted trips. Drivers stamp in bursts. The 5th percentile of slack is +2.5 minutes, comfortably positive, so the feasibility test runs with zero added tolerance. Both numbers are calibrated in calibration.json, not chosen.

Ninety junctions sit in between: too fast for legal speeds, possible at the physical ceiling. They stay merged but flagged, and the explorer draws them dotted with a tooltip. A further 133 pairs of tracks are continuations (same crew starting a new identifier within 20 minutes and 3 km of closing the old one, same route prefix); the later trip carries a "continues from" note. All 13,365 tracks, phantoms included, were routed on the graph with zero failures.

Placing the unstamped readings

142,999 rows are fill readings with no button press. Each was offered to every track that passes its bin at a plausible moment, and charged the legal-time detour its insertion would cost. A reading joins a track as tier I only when the best candidate beats the runner-up by at least 70/30 on that cost; otherwise it stays tier L with a question mark. Readings feasible for no logged track at all went to a separate pool: 11,398 of them. Chained by the same feasibility rules, they form 1,674 phantom tracks (reconstructed vehicles of three stops or more, absorbing 10,136 readings) plus 1,262 isolated observations. Phantom tracks are the strongest single finding of the rebuild: bins were verifiably visited by vehicles that never appear in the logbook.

Limitation. The 2,981 evicted placements show that greedy insertion is imperfect; they are retained row-by-row in dropped_v7.parquet. And a low-confidence (L) placement is a guess between plausible tracks, which is why the explorer never lets one masquerade as evidence.

3Cleaning the sensor file

The raw sensor export is one long table: bin, timestamp, fill value. A quarter of it cannot be used as fill information. Negative values are error codes, not levels; frozen electronics repeat one number for weeks; occasional readings jump upward faster than any bin can physically fill. Each pathology has a written rule, and every removed reading keeps its reason in sensor_removed.parquet.

Removal rules, applied in order

The hardware clearly improved. Error codes made up 36.6% of all 2020 readings and 2.6% of 2024 readings, and a typical bin went from one report every two days to about 1.4 per day (fleet means run higher, up to 5.7 in early 2024, because some 2023-fitted units report many times a day).

Eras and the ceiling problem

The sensor's unit was never documented, and its behaviour changed twice: the scale shifted in November 2020, and the reporting rate doubled from 2023. The record is therefore split into three eras (E1 to October 2020, E2 November 2020 – December 2022, E3 from January 2023), and each bin's fill is expressed as a percentage of that bin's own maximum within the era. The ceiling is remarkably uniform: median 82, maximum 84, in all three eras. Whether "82" means a physically full bin or the sensor's blind zone is an open question (registered as such since round W2), which is exactly why the explorer shows percentages of ceiling rather than claiming litres.

Detecting emptyings in the sensor record

An emptying shows up as a sharp fall. The rule: a clean fall of at least 25 units within at most 24 hours. 50,900 such falls were found; 3,357 were discarded because a removed reading sat inside the fall window (the fall might be an artefact of the fault), leaving 47,543 detected emptying events. Each carries a confidence from the window's width: high within 6 h (14,864 after demotion), medium within 12 h (6,447), low otherwise (26,232). One further guard: if the level rebounds by 20 points within 6 hours of the fall, the event smells of a sensor dip rather than a truck, and its confidence is demoted to low. That demotion touched 21,055 events, so the confident core is deliberately small.

4What each file says about the other

With the driver file rebuilt (section 2) and the sensor file cleaned (section 3), the two can finally be laid side by side. The matching rule is simple: a stamped stop counts as sensor-confirmed when a detected emptying of the same bin overlaps the stamp by at most 90 minutes. The comparison is only fair where a sensor was actually reporting, so the table below counts stamped stops at bins whose sensor delivered at least one clean reading in that calendar year.

Three things follow. The overlap is thin: of 60,477 stamped emptyings, 9,515 have a matching sensor event, and 28,662 more sit at bins with no live sensor that year at all. 2020 barely registers on the sensor side because cadence was one reading every two days and a third of readings were error codes; a fall cannot be seen between two readings that never arrived. And the sensor-only column is large in every mature year.

The headline: 37,784 emptyings appear in the sensor record and nowhere in the logbook. Valued at each bin's volume, its level before the fall and mid-range material densities, that is roughly 4,500 tonnes of collected recyclables with no written trace. The explorer draws these as diamonds (38,419 placements on 6,638 tracks, since one event can sit within 300 m of two different trips that day).

Honesty about that tonnage figure: it is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a measurement. It inherits the density bands, the unverified fill unit and the era ceilings, and none of it was weighed. The event count is the solid number; the tonnes are its rough translation. Earlier rounds also showed record-level agreement between driver fill notes and sensor levels stuck near 50%, so the two instruments are treated throughout as non-interchangeable witnesses rather than as calibrations of each other. Consistent with all of this, recorded truck kilometres run about three times the shortest path through the logged stops (a W4 result), so the logbook is best read as a hard lower bound on the real work done.

Standing circuits

If crews drive habitual rounds, trips should repeat their site sets. Among the 3,164 eligible trips (at least 4 sites, no batch entries, no speed-flagged junctions), chronological clustering by Jaccard similarity (the share of sites two trips have in common) at a 0.5 threshold finds 151 standing circuits containing 1,103 trips, 34.9% of the eligible set. The threshold sits at the coverage/cohesion elbow and reads as "majority of sites shared with the founding trip". Coverage falls from 59.8% of 2020 trips to zero in early 2024: the operation visibly moved away from fixed rounds, which is what a sensor-driven service is supposed to do.
